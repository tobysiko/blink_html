# Why some objectives are harder — and why points are the wrong fix

## First, a correction to everything I have quoted

My per-objective achievement rates have been measured on 20–30 samples. At that
size the 95% confidence interval is roughly **±20 percentage points**, which is
wider than the differences I was using them to justify. Coastal Chain read 43%
in one run and 17% in the next.

Measured properly, two objectives per batch, ~60–120 samples each:

| Objective | Samples | Achieved | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Great Range — three connected mountains | 60 | **46.7%** | 34–59 |
| Coastal Chain — plains-ocean-plains | 120 | **38.3%** | 30–47 |
| Deep Wood — three connected forest | 60 | **20.0%** | 10–30 |
| Open Sea — three connected ocean | 120 | **18.3%** | 11–25 |

Those intervals still overlap in places, but the mountain/forest gap is real:
46.7% against 20.0%, non-overlapping.

## You were right about capacity — my explanation was wrong

I said forest holds two units so "capacity works against the shape". That was
nonsense: nothing requires a tile to fill before you explore another one. A
player chasing Deep Wood simply places forest tiles.

**The real cause is the starting map.**

| Terrain | A same-terrain triangle exists on the final map |
|---|---|
| mountain | **100% of games** |
| ocean | 15% |
| forest | 10% |
| plains | 0% |

The three-player starting map is **itself a mountain triangle** — the mountains
at (1,3), (2,2), (2,3) are mutually adjacent. Mountain Fortress is not built,
it is *occupied*. And it is worse than that: it is player-count dependent. The
two-player start has two mountains and the four-player start has four in a
stair, and neither contains a mutually adjacent triple.

So the disparity was never forest-versus-mountain. It was **seeded-versus-built**.

## Should higher points compensate?

No, and the deal-two-keep-one mechanic is what settles it.

A player holding a 47% card and a 20% card keeps the 47% card. To make the 20%
card a real choice, its value would need to be roughly **2.3× higher** — about
9 points against 4. Against a typical final score of 22–26, a single 9-point
objective would decide games on the draw.

So compensating by points does not create a decision; it creates a lottery, and
it makes the objective the largest single scoring event in the game. The cards
have to be **close in difficulty**, not compensated for being far apart.

## What I would do instead

**1. Drop the single-terrain mountain objectives.** Any three-mountain shape is
partly gifted by setup at three players and not at two or four. That is the
worst kind of variance — it changes with player count, which a player cannot
read from the card.

**2. Build the deck from mixed-terrain chains.** They are the only family that
measured consistently, because a chain of three different terrains needs one
tile of each — which fits an explore budget of about two per suit exactly. The
three chain objectives are the ones to keep.

**3. If a single-terrain card is wanted for flavour, use "three connected",
not a triangle or a line**, and expect roughly 20%. Deep Wood and Open Sea can
stay at that rate *if* they are priced at 5 against the chains' 4 — a 25%
premium for roughly half the chance is defensible, where a 130% premium is not.

**4. Fix the seeding rather than the scoring.** If mountain objectives are
wanted, the three-player starting map needs mountains that are not mutually
adjacent — but with three contiguous hexes the only options are a triangle or a
line, so one mountain objective will always be seeded. That is a reason to keep
single-terrain objectives away from the two terrains the setup places.

## The honest bottom line

The four chain objectives sit at 32–48% across every measurement I have taken.
Everything else is either seeded by setup or lands near 20% with a wide
interval. A six-card deck of mixed-terrain chains would be the most balanced
thing I can support with the evidence — and it would give up some of the
thematic range, which is a real cost worth weighing.
