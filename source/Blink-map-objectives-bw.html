Map Objectives

A secret shape to build toward

Once the base game feels familiar, add this layer. Every player is dealt a secret goal for the shape of their world — a river running down from the highlands, a range of peaks, a stretch of coast — and spends the game quietly steering their expansion toward it. The map stops being a place to spread and becomes a place to compose.

01

Setup & scoring

  1. Deal two, keep one. Shuffle the objective cards and deal two to each player, face down. Look at both, keep one, and return the other to the box unseen. Choose before the card draft if you can — the objective you keep should inform the hand you build.
  2. Keep it hidden. Your objective stays secret for the whole game. Nobody is told what anyone is chasing.
  3. Reveal together. At the end of the game, after the base score is counted, every player turns their objective face up at the same moment and adds its points if they completed it.
Completed or not — there is no half

An objective scores its full value once, or nothing. You cannot score it twice by building the pattern twice, and there is no penalty for failing. Every pattern is exactly three tiles, and every tile must be one you occupy — a tile with at least one of your units standing on it. Empty tiles, and tiles held only by a rival, never count.

Every objective is worth 4 points. For scale, a finished game usually scores somewhere between twenty and thirty, so an objective is worth roughly a fifth of a good result — enough to steer a game, not enough to decide one on its own.

All twelve ask for the same thing in different terrain: three tiles you occupy in a chain, the middle one touching both ends. They are alike in shape so that the choice you make when you keep one is about your hand, not about which card is easiest. If you drafted Ocean, the Fjord and the Lookout are close; if you drafted Plains, the Mountain Pass is.

Not yet re-tuned for v0.21 — play the base game first

This deck was balanced against the v0.20 placement rules, where a meld landed as a connected pattern. Under v0.21 the map ends about 20% smaller and players hold fewer tiles, so the chains are harder across the board — and no longer evenly hard. Measured passively, they now range from about 1% to 28% where they once sat in a much narrower band, and Mountain Pass, Coastal Chain and Clearing have all but stopped happening.

Treat the twelve as a draft. They are still playable — nothing here contradicts the base rules — but the promise that all twelve are equally reachable is not currently true, and the flat 4 points needs revisiting once v0.21 has been played.

Twelve cards is enough for four players to be dealt two each with four still unseen, so no table ever sees the whole deck.

Building in the open

Your objective is secret; your map is not. Every chain uses ordinary, useful ground, so the early tiles of one look like plain expansion — but the third tile is the tell. A rival who guesses right can settle the space you needed, and a chain broken at the last tile is worth nothing. Build the ambiguous end first.

03

Going further

Two variants once the layer is second nature:

  • Open objectives. Deal two face up beside the map, shared by everyone, each player scoring it if they build it. A gentler, more readable game — good for teaching the patterns.
  • Keep both. Deal two and keep both, each still worth its full value. More points in play and a broader target, at the cost of some focus.
